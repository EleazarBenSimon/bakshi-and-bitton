Mandatory order

HCJ 8987/22

Movement for Quality (Abolition of) Government in Israel et al. v. Minister of Justice et al. (consolidated petitions: HCJ 99/23, HCJ 179/23, HCJ 1711/24, HCJ 896/24)

Here the Court didn't grab power so much as protect its own ranks: three justices whose own appointments flow through that very committee (Willner, Grosskopf, Stein) unanimously ordered Justice Minister Yariv Levin to convene the Judicial Selection Committee and elect a permanent Supreme Court President. The absolute order gave him 14 days to publish the candidate list. The reasoning: section 7(a) of the Courts Law makes convening a duty, and Levin's demand for 'broad consensus' as a precondition hollowed the statute out. The judiciary itself set the timetable for staffing its own bench.

3 justicesVote 3–0
Why this matters
Here the Court seized the timetable for staffing its own bench. Three justices whose appointments run through that very Judicial Selection Committee — Willner, Grosskopf, Stein — unanimously ordered the Justice Minister, a minister of the elected government, to convene the Committee and elect a permanent Supreme Court President, with an absolute order giving him 14 days to publish the candidate list. The authority reclaimed was taken not from the Knesset but from the executive: the Minister's discretion over when and how to convene was converted into a schedule the judiciary set for itself, on the reasoning that section 7(a) of the Courts Law makes convening a duty rather than a choice. Yet — against the escalating pattern — when the Minister did not comply within the window, the Court declined to hold him in contempt, calling that tool reserved for extreme circumstances only; it reaffirmed the mandatory order and extended the deadline, but withheld the sharper sanction.

Defiance signals
Following the September 12, 2024 mandatory order, Levin did not publish the candidate list or convene the Committee for a presidential vote within the 14-day window. On December 12, 2024, the High Court held a follow-up hearing on a petition to find Levin in contempt; the panel (including Acting President Amit) declined to hold Levin in contempt, characterizing contempt as reserved for extreme circumstances and noting some procedural steps had been taken, but ordered that a vote on a permanent Supreme Court president be held in the Committee by January 16, 2025. The Committee convened and elected Justice Yitzhak Amit as President on January 26, 2025; Levin was absent from that session. Notes

Structural decision: entered as a SINGLE ruling keyed to the primary mandatory order of September 12, 2024 (HCJ 8987/22 leads a cluster of consolidated petitions including HCJ 99/23, HCJ 179/23, HCJ 1711/24, HCJ 896/24). The December 12, 2024 follow-up ruling — which declined to hold Levin in contempt but issued an additional deadline — is documented in defiance_signals rather than as a separate ruling, because (a) it is procedurally subordinate to the September 12 mandatory order (a compliance-enforcement step within the same proceeding rather than an independent disposition on the merits), and (b) it would not be analytically parallel to the project's other 'Court declined' entry (Zini, 427/10/25), where the Court declined to intervene on the merits of an appointment; here the Court declined a remedial tool while reaffirming the underlying mandatory order. Panel composition varied across sittings in the cluster: the September 12, 2024 absolute order was issued by Willner, Grosskopf, and Stein; the December 12, 2024 follow-up panel included Acting President Yitzhak Amit and additional justices. Vote was unanimous on substance across all sittings. Compliance was achieved on January 26, 2025, when the Committee convened and elected Justice Amit as President; Levin did not attend that session. The official URL points to a court document filed January 2, 2025 within the case file (NetVerdicts/2025/1/2/2022-0-8987-62-2), which appears to be a procedural record in the 8987/22 docket. Doctrine: 'ultra_vires' is used here for the Court's finding that the Minister failed to exercise a mandatory statutory power under section 7(a) of the Courts Law (closest mapping in the controlled vocabulary to ministerial-duty-to-exercise-statutory-authority); 'reasonableness' captures the Court's secondary analysis of the Minister's stated policy of conditioning convening on broad consensus. Separation-of-powers and judicial-independence framings discussed in the underlying petitions are not separately enumerable in the project's doctrine vocabulary.
